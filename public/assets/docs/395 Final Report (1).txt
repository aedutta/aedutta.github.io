                                            Abstract

We designed and built a handheld controller PCB that pairs with a quadcopter to provide
wireless flight control. The controller is centered on an STM32L031K6T6 microcontroller that
samples two analog COM-09032 thumb-joysticks via its on-chip ADC, reads orientation data
from a Bosch BMI270 IMU over I²C, and transmits information to the drone over a sub-GHz link
using a TI CC1101 RF transceiver. The transceiver was originally designed around the 433 MHz
but switched to 315 MHz to better match the circuit components and antenna we had on hand.
During our demo, our PCB had short circuited and we were not able to fully show the work we
did over this semester. This report covers the system architecture, hardware design, firmware
design, lessons learned from a number of build issues we encountered, and recommendations
for future ECE 395 teams attempting a similar controller project.

Introduction

Overview

The overall goal of the project was to implement something that combined communication and
motors in a fun and interesting way. After deliberation, the four of us decided to make a drone
and then subsequently split into two groups. This group's job was to design and build a
handheld controller to interface with the other groups' drone project. After brainstorming, our
controller was designed to have analog joystick and tilt input and send the data over the
433MHz band (this was later tweaked to the 315MHz band).

Understanding the Drone

To understand the design behind the controller, you must first understand the design of the
drone that it will be controlling. The main body of the drone is a PCB to limit the amount of
mechanical engineering that needs to get involved. Uses the STM32L03KT6T6 as its MCU in
order to control its motor control ICs (DRV8311P), BNO086 IMU, and CC1101 RF transceiver.
The big overview is that the MCU first initializes all circuits, then enters its main loop where it
receives controller data from the CC1101. Using this controller data, the MCU will send
instructions to the DRV8311Ps to move the motors.

System Overview

The controller is organized into three subsystems: the MCU and its supporting power and
programming circuitry, the sub-GHz RF transmit chain (CC1101 plus L/C network and antenna),
and the user-input front end (two analog joysticks plus the BMI270 IMU). The STM32 sits at the
center, reading user input on one side and driving the radio on the other.
The joystick and IMU sampling are decoupled from the radio. This keeps the main loop simple
and predictable, and means the radio is the only thing that can stall the loop.

Board Operation

To use the controller, connect a USB-C cable from any 5 V source (laptop, phone charger, USB
battery bank) to J2. Within power-up, the firmware finishes its init sequence and will start
transmitting at the 50 Hz packet rate.
The two joysticks are positioned for thumb operation, in the standard "game controller" layout:
left stick controls throttle (Y) and yaw (X), right stick controls pitch (Y) and roll (X). The pilot can
additionally tilt the entire controller to bias the drone's attitude.
To program the board, connect an ST-LINK V2 or V3 to the 10-pin SWD header (J1) with the
ST-LINK's 3.3 V output enabled. The board will draw negligible current on the SWD-only power
path, so a USB-C cable is not required for flashing.

Power/GND

The board accepts +5 V from USB-C (J2) and uses a low-dropout linear regulator (U2) to
produce the +3.3 V VDD rail that powers the MCU, the CC1101, the BMI270, and the joystick
potentiometer ladders. A series of bulk and high-frequency decoupling capacitors (C1­C3,
C22­C23) are distributed around the regulator and around each active IC. Each VDD/VDDA pin
on the STM32 has its own 100 nF cap placed within a few millimeters of the pin, with a 4.7 µF
bulk cap nearby.

Ground is a flood pour on the bottom layer with stitching vias around the RF section to keep the
antenna return path short. The CC1101's ground pad is tied to the pour with a 2x2 via array.

Microcontroller (STM32L031K6T6)

The STM32L031K6T6 is an ultra-low-power Cortex-M0+ part in a 32-pin LQFP with 32 KB of
flash and 8 KB of SRAM. We picked it for three reasons: it is the same MCU the drone team
selected and we used before in class (so we could share a CC1101 driver), its on-chip ADC is
more than fast enough to sample two joysticks at our packet rate, and its SPI1 peripheral
comfortably hits the 6.5 MHz the CC1101 tolerates on SCLK.

Communications

The CC1101 is a single-chip sub-GHz transceiver from TI. The MCU drives it over SPI1, with
two additional digital lines (CSn and GDO0). GDO0 is configured to assert at end-of-packet,
which we wire to an STM32 EXTI line so the firmware can sleep between TXes.

Originally the link was specified at 433.92 MHz, and the matching network around the CC1101
(L1­L4 and C13­C16) was laid out using the values from TI's 433 MHz reference design:
L1/L2/L4 = 27 nH, L3 = 22 nH, C13/C14 = 3.9 pF, C15 = 8.2 pF, C16 = 5.6 pF. When we
populated and tested the board, the return loss at 433 MHz was poor and we could not close
the link reliably.

Rather than respin the PCB, we tried reworked the matching network in place to values that,
combined with the quarter-wave whip antenna AE1 we had on hand, produced a noticeably
cleaner match at 315 MHz: L1/L2/L4 = 33 nH, L3 = 18 nH, C13/C14 = 6.8 pF, C15 = 12 pF, C16
= 6.8 pF. C12 and C17 (220 pF) act as RF DC-blocking caps on either side of the network and
were left unchanged. We then reprogrammed the CC1101's base-frequency registers

(FREQ2/FREQ1/FREQ0) for 315 MHz and verified link operation at that band. The 315 MHz
ISM allocation in the US permits short-range device operation under FCC Part 15, which is
appropriate for a tethered demo project.

Y1 is the 26.0 MHz SMD-3225 crystal used by the CC1101 PLL, with C19 and C20 (both 27 pF)
as load caps. R5 (56 k, RBIAS) sets the internal current reference.

Joystick Input (COM-09032 × 2)

Each COM-09032 module from SparkFun is a Nintendo-style thumb-joystick: two orthogonal
potentiometers (one for X, one for Y) to VDD/GND, plus a pushbutton that closes when the user
clicks the stick straight down.

Both joysticks share VDD and GND with the rest of the board. Their four wipers run to
PA0­PA3, which the STM32's on-chip 12-bit SAR ADC samples in scan mode. The two
pushbutton outputs go to PA4 and PB6 and read as active-low GPIOs.

Gyroscope / IMU Input (BMI270)

The BMI270 is a Bosch 6-axis IMU (3-axis accelerometer + 3-axis gyroscope) with an internal
DSP. On the controller we use it strictly for tilt input: the firmware reads the accelerometer at 100
Hz over I²C, computes roll and pitch from the gravity vector, and folds those two angles into the
outgoing packet alongside the joystick values. The pilot can use this to bias the drone's attitude
by tilting the controller itself, which is more intuitive than fine joystick movement.

Overview

The firmware running on the STM32 is responsible for initializing/configuring the CC1101,
BMI270, reading joystick/sensor inputs, stuffing the data into a packet, and sending the data
with the antenna.

STM Configuration

ADC_IN0: This ADC is used to read in analog voltage from the left joystick horizontal direction
and turn it into digital signals to then send in a packet
ADC_IN1: This ADC is used to read in analog voltage from the left joystick vertical direction and
turn it into digital signals to then send in a packet
ADC_IN2: This ADC is used to read in analog voltage from the right joystick horizontal direction

ADC_IN3: This ADC is used to read in analog voltage from the right joystick vertical direction

GPIO_CS: This pin is used to tell the CC1101 when it is being communicated with

I2C1_SCL: This is the clock signal used to synchronize with the IMU
SYS_SWCLK: This was used as a reference clock signal

Main Loop

This code first creates a buffer to be used throughout the main loop. Then it reads in the analog
joystick data using Read_ADC_4Ch and puts it into the buffer. It then reads the acceleration
data and puts that in the same buffer. Then we pack the buffer into a different one. This is done
more to standardize how all of the data is stored and also so that we are sending bytes at a
time. Then we send the packet and introduce a small delay before we loop again.

Conclusion

Issues

Honestly, the biggest issue we ran into was pacing. We spent too long on the schematic and
PCB iteration in the first half of the semester and did not start firmware bring-up in parallel on a
development board as quickly. By the time the PCB came back from fabrication we were trying
to debug the CC1101, the joystick ADC chain, and the BMI270 configuration upload all at once,
which compounded every issue we hit.
Our board was also programmable and tested on a development board, but when soldering on
the PCB components, we had our PCB short circuit (connectivity in one resistor area which led
to our board operating at a third of the original voltage) and were unable to respin another board
in time.

What could be done in the future?

    · Re-spin the RF matching network for 433 MHz with the L/C values pulled directly from
         the TI CC1101 reference design rather than improvised from what was in the lab.

    · Add a small OLED to the controller so the pilot can see live joystick / IMU / link-quality
         values without a USB serial cable.

    · Add a LiPo charger (MCP73831 or similar) and a battery so the controller is actually
         wireless on the controller side as well.

    · Implement bidirectional link with telemetry on the return path.
    · Implement more efficient communication with error correction/detection

Tips for future projects regarding drone controllers

     Starting as early as possible. We spent so much time trying to make the right design for
         our PCB that we delayed ordering one until the last submission day. This basically had
         us stuck to that one PCB and if it didn't work, we couldn't re-order another one.

     Doing work in progress on a dev board. While we were waiting for our PCB, we should
         have spent more time playing with the dev board and the firmware and ensuring that our
         code and project at least work on a breadboard.

     Add test points and LED indicators in your board. Every SPI line, every I²C line, every
         supply rail, and the CC1101 GDO0 pin should have a labeled test point.

     Talk with Michael more.

Final Remarks

In its previous state, the controller successfully sampled both joysticks and the IMU, packed
their values into an 8-byte payload, and transmitted them over a 315 MHz CC1101 link to the
drone. The two halves were integrated late enough in the semester that we did not get a fully
airborne demo, but the wireless link itself is functional and the controller PCB is a usable
platform for future ECE 395 teams to build on. Most of what slowed us down was schedule
discipline rather than any single technical problem, and we hope the advice above helps the
next group avoid the same trap.
